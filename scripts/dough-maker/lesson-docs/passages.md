# Passages

This file covers how to divide the Bible into passages and give each one a type. It does not pick stations. That has its own file.

## The text

- Work from the **BSB** (Berean Standard Bible).
- The BSB's section headings can be used as hints for where a passage starts and stops. They are never the final word. Use judgment.

## The whole Bible

- **About 1,500 passages** across the whole Bible. Anywhere from 1,200 to 2,100 is fine.
- **Every verse belongs to exactly one passage.** No verse is skipped, and no verse is in two passages.
- **Each passage is one unbroken range,** from a start verse to an end verse.
- **Every passage has exactly one type.**

## What makes a passage

A passage is one complete unit the baker can understand on its own. For example:

- a story episode
- a whole psalm
- a speech or sermon
- one step of a letter's argument
- one prophetic message

## Where to start and stop

- **Break at natural turns:** a new scene (the place, the time or the people change), a new speaker or speech, a new psalm, or the end of one argument and the start of the next.
- **Chapter breaks are hints, not rules.** Chapters were added long after the Bible was written, and some cut a story in half.
- **Never split** in the middle of a conversation or a sentence.
- **A long speech can be split** where it moves to a new topic, never in the middle of a thought. For example, the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7) is too long for one passage.
- **Never mix types in one passage.** A passage breaks wherever the type changes. A song or prayer that stays with its story (see below) doesn't count as a type change.

## Keep these together

- **A parable and its explanation stay in the same passage,** with everything between them. For example, Matthew 13:1–23 holds the parable of the sower, the disciples asking why Jesus speaks in parables, and His explanation.
- **A song or prayer that needs the story around it stays with that story,** and the passage takes the story's type. For example, Moses' song (Exodus 15:1–21) makes sense only after the crossing of the Red Sea, so it stays with the story as Narrative. Jonah's prayer from inside the fish (Jonah 2) stays with Jonah's story.

## Length

- **Long enough for a full lesson.** Prep needs real moments to ask about, and Burnt Cookies needs 8 lines that retell the passage.
- **Short enough to stay bite-sized.** Prep tops out around 20 choice cards, and its story cards shouldn't pile up between them. If Prep would have to rush past important details to fit, the passage is too long.
- **Long stretches can run longer.** A genealogy, a census or a run of everyday laws is summed up in one story card in Prep, so it doesn't need to be split up just for length. Key laws, like the Ten Commandments, are not long stretches.

## When to merge

- When a unit is too short to fill a lesson, merge it with the passage **right before it or right after it**, whichever one it connects to more. For example, Psalm 117 is 2 verses, so it merges with Psalm 116 or Psalm 118.
- Merge only when both are the **same type** and truly connect.
- **If a short unit can't truly join either neighbor, it stands alone.** A standalone passage can be a little thinner than the goals: fewer choice cards, and as few as 1 station. Never merge unrelated content just to reach length. For example, Jesus rejected at Nazareth (Matthew 13:53–58) is 6 verses. The parables before it are a different scene, and Herod and John the Baptist come after it, so it stands alone.

## When to split

- **A long unit gets split where its theme changes.** For example, Psalm 119 is 176 verses. Split it into groups of stanzas wherever the theme shifts.
- **If the theme doesn't change, keep it whole,** as long as it can still fit in one Prep.

## Types

**The type comes from what the passage itself is, never from the book it's in.** Read the passage and ask what it mostly is. The book is never the answer.

| Type | The passage is mostly… |
|---|---|
| **Narrative** | events happening to people, one after another: who did what, then what happened |
| **Poetry & Wisdom** | a song, a poetic prayer, proverbs or wisdom sayings |
| **Letters** | a writer teaching or arguing in a letter to a church or a person |
| **Prophetic** | a prophet delivering God's message: warnings, promises or visions |

### When books mix types

| Passage | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Isaiah 36–39 (Hezekiah and the Assyrian army) | Narrative | A story inside a prophet's book |
| Jonah 1 (Jonah runs, the storm) | Narrative | Events happening to people, inside a prophet's book |
| Exodus 15:1–21 (the Song of Moses) | Narrative | A song that needs its story stays with it |
| Peter's speech at Pentecost (Acts 2) | Narrative | A speech inside a story is part of the story |
| Job 1–2 and 42:7–17 (Job loses everything, then is restored) | Narrative | The story around Job's poetry. The poetry between them is Poetry & Wisdom |

### Rulings

These rulings override the type test above.

- Lamentations is **Poetry & Wisdom**.
- Daniel 1–6 is **Narrative**. Daniel 7–12 is **Prophetic**.
- Revelation 2–3, the letters to the churches, is **Prophetic**.

## Titles

- Short and plain. Say what the passage is about. For example, "Jonah Runs from the LORD."
- Only use what the text states. Nothing added.

## Output for each passage

- Title
- Start verse
- End verse
- Type
