# Station: Idioms

This file covers the content for the Idioms station.

## What it is

Papa Dorin presents a phrase people still say today that comes from the passage. The baker sees how it's used now, finds what it meant in the passage, and then spots someone using it wrong.

## The passage text

- The passage text is provided in the **BSB** (Berean Standard Bible).
- Every true fact and every verse number comes from the BSB.

## The idioms

- The idioms for the passage are found by the station picker, and the pipeline saves them in the database. Use the ones given.
- If there is more than one, each idiom gets the full flow before the next one starts.

## Who's talking

- Papa Dorin presents every question. His voice comes from his own file.
- **Written for adults.** No scenarios about school, parents or anything aimed at kids. Kids can still play.

## The flow for each idiom

1. **Today.** A modern line uses the idiom. The baker picks what it means.
2. **Then.** Papa Dorin takes the baker to the scene in the passage the idiom comes from. The baker picks what it meant there.
3. **Follow-up.** *Optional.* See below.
4. **Spot the misuse.** Three sentences use the idiom. Two use it right and one uses it wrong. The baker picks the one that uses it wrong.

## The follow-up

A follow-up is one more Then question, asked only when the passage says something right around the idiom that adds to what it means. It teaches the part of the idiom's Bible meaning that today's use leaves out.

- **Ask one** when the passage adds to the idiom's meaning. For example, people today use "a wolf in sheep's clothing" to mean someone who looks harmless but isn't. Jesus goes further and tells how to spot them: "By their fruit you will recognize them" (Matthew 7:16).
- **Don't ask one** when nothing near the idiom adds to its meaning. The flow is then Today, Then, Spot the misuse.
- A follow-up is never a general question about the passage. That's Prep's job.
- **One follow-up at most** per idiom.

## Answer rules

- Each question has **3 answers** and **one try**.
- All three answers are the same kind of thing, about the same length and the same tone. None are silly.
- Each wrong answer must be false for that moment.
- In Spot the misuse, the right answer is the sentence that uses the idiom wrong. The other two must use it correctly.
- The code shuffles where the right answer sits. The agent doesn't need to.

## Output for each question

- The question
- The 3 answers
- The right answer
- The verse that backs it. Today and Spot the misuse can have none.

## Cookies

- Every right answer adds **1 mango cookie** to the box.
- A wrong answer adds nothing.

## Example: a wolf in sheep's clothing (Matthew 7)

1. **Today:** "A coworker leans over and says, 'Careful with the new investor. He's a wolf in sheep's clothing.' What does she mean?"
   - A. He's shy but acts tough
   - **B. He seems harmless but means harm**
   - C. He changes his mind a lot
2. **Then:** "Jesus said, 'Beware of false prophets. They come to you in sheep's clothing, but inwardly they are ravenous wolves.' Who was He warning about?"
   - A. Shepherds who secretly steal from their own flocks
   - B. Wild wolves that hunt sheep in the hills
   - **C. False prophets who look harmless but are dangerous** *(Matthew 7:15)*
3. **Follow-up:** "So how does Jesus say to recognize them?"
   - **A. By their fruit, by what they actually do** *(Matthew 7:16)*
   - B. By the clothes they wear
   - C. By how many people follow them
4. **Spot the misuse:** "Which one uses it wrong?"
   - A. "That 'free gift card' text was a wolf in sheep's clothing. It stole my password."
   - **B. "My neighbor is a wolf in sheep's clothing. He's kind and always helps out."**
   - C. "That charity was a wolf in sheep's clothing. The money never reached anyone."
